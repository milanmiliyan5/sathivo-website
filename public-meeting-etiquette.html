Public venue etiquette · Practical guidance

Public-place etiquette for a relaxed first meetup

A pleasant platonic plan works best when the location, timing, spending and personal boundaries are clear. Here is how to be considerate in cafés, bookstores and other shared spaces without treating strangers or staff as part of the booking.

By Sathivo · Updated 21 November 2026 · 18+ · Strictly platonic

1. Pick a public place suited to the activity

A venue should make the agreed plan practical, not just look good in photographs. A café is suitable for a conversation if noise and seating are manageable. A bookstore may suit browsing but not a long, loud discussion. A museum or library may have specific rules about food, talking and taking pictures. Check the venue's official information before making plans, especially if you're unfamiliar with the area.

Choose a visible entrance and a place other people use regularly. A business being open to the public is not proof that every area inside is appropriate. Avoid secluded back rooms or private spaces for a first meeting. If accessibility, quiet seating or drinking water matters, confirm the options directly with the venue rather than assuming they exist.

2. Be clear about spending before you arrive

Two people can agree to meet in a café while having very different expectations about buying food. Neither person should be surprised by a large bill. A considerate request might say, “We can each pay for our own drink,” or “I can cover the two tickets, but not other purchases.” Do not assume a companion must buy something, accept gifts or split costs unless that was discussed and accepted.

On Sathivo, a companion chooses a displayed hourly rate and a requester may propose a different amount. If the proposed rate is accepted, that still does not silently cover tickets, travel, meals or extra time. Work out those additional costs before meeting. Companion booking transfers take place directly between the participants, not through Sathivo.

3. Respect the venue and the people working there

Order only what you're comfortable paying for, follow posted rules, and be considerate about table occupancy during busy periods. In a quiet library, keep conversations brief or move to an appropriate public common area. In a bookstore, put books back carefully and leave room for other customers. At a public event, follow photography, queueing and entry requirements.

Service staff are not there to resolve personal booking disagreements. If a conversation becomes uncomfortable, end the meeting calmly and move away. If you need immediate safety assistance, follow the venue's own security procedures or contact appropriate emergency services rather than creating a public confrontation.

4. Arrive with an independent plan

Confirm the meeting point, start time and a realistic way home. “Outside the main entrance at 3 PM” is easier to locate than “somewhere near the mall.” If the venue has multiple entrances, name the one you mean. Keep your phone charged and plan your own transport; you shouldn't have to rely on someone you've just met to take you home.

If you're delayed, communicate as soon as practical. A short message such as “The bus is running late; I may arrive ten minutes after our agreed time. Does that still work for you?” is more respectful than leaving someone wondering. The other person is allowed to say no to an unexpected delay or change.

5. Keep conversation friendly, not intrusive

Shared interests, the activity or the venue provide natural topics. Ask about a book section, museum display, board game or public event before personal matters. Avoid pressing for someone's home address, family background, employment, private social accounts or financial situation. A person being paid for time does not owe private disclosures or agreement with your opinions.

Not every meeting needs continuous talking. Browsing an exhibition quietly can be enjoyable if both participants chose that activity. If you sense the other person prefers a quieter experience, you can ask, “Would you rather explore without talking for a few minutes?” See the conversation starters guide for relaxed examples.

6. Ask before photographing anyone

Some people enjoy taking pictures of an outing, but that does not mean they want their face photographed, location tagged or messages posted. Ask permission before including another person in a photo or sharing anything publicly. Respect venue rules about camera use as well. A photo taken for personal memories is not automatically approved for a social media post.

Do not share private chat screenshots, booking details or someone's contact information to describe an interaction. If there is a genuine safety issue, use the reporting process and share only the necessary factual details with the authorized support team.

7. Keep physical boundaries platonic

Sathivo is an adults-only, strictly platonic platform. Conversation or a paid activity is not an invitation to touch, flirt or make sexual requests. Let the other person choose their preferred distance, and ask if an action such as taking a group picture would be comfortable. If someone says no, accept it immediately rather than trying to bargain.

Both sides may stop or change their participation when uncomfortable. If you wish to extend the activity, ask before the original end time and clarify any extra cost; no one should feel trapped because a ticket was purchased or because another participant travelled to the venue.

8. Respect time limits and reasonable cancellations

Agree on a start and end time before the meeting. A movie may run longer than the booked duration, while a bookstore visit may finish sooner. If your plan changes, discuss the new timing explicitly. If either person cannot attend, use the available cancellation flow and communicate politely where appropriate. Do not assume a booking status automatically means a payment succeeded or a refund is due.

“Thanks for meeting; I'll need to leave at the agreed time” is a complete closing statement. You can part without promising another booking, exchanging phone numbers or sharing a private location. Read the booking status guide for practical next steps when plans are cancelled.

9. A practical one-hour café example

An illustrative example—not an actual booking

Two adults agree to a one-hour public café conversation at an accepted hourly offer of ₹250. They meet at the entrance at 4 PM and intend to finish at 5 PM. Each buys their own drink, uses their own transport and chooses not to take photos. When the hour finishes, both thank each other and leave independently. Nothing in this example guarantees availability, a particular rate or an outcome.

The details matter more than a polished script. Real people may have accessibility requirements, travel delays or different budgets. Ask politely and keep room for a genuine no.

10. Before you leave home

  • Is this a lawful, public location with suitable opening hours?
  • Have you agreed on the activity, time and an obvious entrance?
  • Who pays the hourly offer, tickets, food and travel?
  • Can each of you travel and leave independently?
  • Have you agreed not to share photos or private details without permission?
  • Do you know how to report unwanted behaviour if needed?
